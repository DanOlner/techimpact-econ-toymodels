# Sources in the GM AI work planner 2025

Every source mentioned in the earlier project's journal (see `gm-ai-planner-2025-tidied.md`), grouped by type, with its link where the journal gives one. Each summary says what point the journal took from the source, not what a fresh reading of it would find.

How much weight to give them: the research papers and reports, and the data and lookups, are solid. Most of the articles, blogs and LinkedIn posts are opinion. They're a good record of the debate, but they aren't evidence. Where the journal has no comment on a source, the summary says so.

## Research papers and reports

- **Acemoglu & Restrepo (2018), "The Race between Man and Machine: Implications of Technology for Growth, Factor Shares, and Employment"**, American Economic Review 108, 1488–1542. <https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20160696> Humans differ from Leontief's horses because they have a comparative advantage in new, more complex tasks, so if new tasks keep being created, employment and the labour share can hold up under rapid automation. The journal sets this against historical evidence that the new jobs went to different people.
- **Bessen, "AI and Jobs: The Role of Demand"** (NBER working paper 24235). <https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w24235/w24235.pdf> Automation cuts prices and can improve products, which raises demand, so employment can grow even as the labour needed per unit of output falls. Once the market saturates, further productivity gains mean job losses, so jobs rise and then fall (the journal calls it a U-shaped curve).
- **Schneider & Vipond, "The Past and Future of Work: How History Can Inform the Age of Automation"** (CESifo working paper 10766). <https://www.ifo.de/DocDL/cesifo1_wp10766.pdf> New jobs did historically replace those lost to labour-saving technology, but access to them depended on gender, age, education and location, and displaced workers often couldn't move into them. The journal's takeaway: the people doing the "new work" aren't necessarily the ones who lost the old work, so displacement can still be massive.
- **Felten, Raj & Seamans (2021), "Occupational, industry, and geographic exposure to artificial intelligence: A novel dataset and its potential uses"**, Strategic Management Journal 42, 2195–2217. <https://sms.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/smj.3286> Source of the AI Occupational Exposure measure (AIOE), built from O\*NET abilities, with industry (AIIE) and county-level versions. Scores are relative (mean 0, SD 1) and adjusted for how broad a range of abilities a job needs. It was the project's main exposure measure. Using it for the UK assumes the UK's occupation mix is similar to the US's.
- **Pizzinelli, Panton, Tavares, Cazzaniga & Li (2023), "Labor Market Exposure to AI: Cross-country Differences and Distributional Implications"** (IMF working paper). No link in the journal. Builds a complementarity measure from O\*NET work contexts and job zones, to capture how likely AI is to replace people or be used under their supervision. This is the complementarity measure that the IMF's 2024 report combines with Felten's exposure.
- **Cazzaniga et al. (2024), "Gen-AI: Artificial Intelligence and the Future of Work"** (IMF Staff Discussion Note 2024/001). <https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/Staff-Discussion-Notes/Issues/2024/01/14/Gen-AI-Artificial-Intelligence-and-the-Future-of-Work-542379> Combines exposure and complementarity to sort occupations into three groups: high exposure and high complementarity, high exposure and low complementarity, and low exposure. On this view judges are exposed but shielded, while clerical workers are at risk. The journal picks out the points about competition: workers can still lose out if their employers don't invest in AI, and early-adopting firms may widen inequality within occupations.
- **Chen, Srinivasan & Zakerinia, "Displacement or Complementarity? The Labor Market Impact of Generative AI"** (Harvard Business School working paper). The journal notes it as covering one of the two axes the project was asked for (complement vs substitute). The HBS paper the journal links elsewhere, described as having an exposure index based on US data from December 2024 (<https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/25-039_05fbec84-1f23-459b-8410-e3cd7ab6c88a.pdf>), is very likely this one. HBS says the paper first came out in December 2024, but I couldn't open the PDF to confirm.
- **Tomlinson, Jaffe, Wang, Counts & Suri (2025), "Working with AI: Measuring the Occupational Implications of Generative AI"** (Microsoft, arXiv). <https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2507.07935> Maps AI conversations to O\*NET work activities, which apply across occupations, rather than to occupation-specific tasks, which cuts noise. To judge whether a conversation got the user's task done, it uses an LLM classifier as well as thumbs-up and thumbs-down ratings.
- **"Lawyering in the Age of Artificial Intelligence"** (SSRN). <https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4626276> AI help improved the quality of legal work unevenly, with the lowest-skilled participants gaining most, but it saved about the same amount of time however fast people were to begin with. The journal reads this as AI taking on more of the cognitive load, so the lawyer's skill mattered relatively less.
- **MIT NANDA, AI report 2025** (archived copy). <https://web.archive.org/web/20250818145714/https://nanda.media.mit.edu/ai_report_2025.pdf> The Register covered it under a "95 percent zero return" headline (<https://www.theregister.com/2025/08/18/generative_ai_zero_return_95_percent/>). The journal quotes a CIO saying that of dozens of demos, maybe one or two were genuinely useful.
- **Department for Education (2023), "The impact of AI on UK jobs and training"**. <https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-impact-of-ai-on-uk-jobs-and-training> Cites ILO work that sorts occupations into "high augmentation" and "high automation", mapped onto UK occupations with an ISCO–SOC 2010 crosswalk. The journal found it interesting but not much help.
- **DSIT, "Artificial intelligence sector study 2023"**. <https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/artificial-intelligence-sector-study-2023> The UK government's study of the AI sector. The journal asks which data sources it used: not The Data City, but perhaps glass.ai.
- **Jorgenson, Ho & Stiroh (2008), "A Retrospective Look at the U.S. Productivity Growth Resurgence"**, Journal of Economic Perspectives 22(1), 3–24. <https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fjep.22.1.3> Linked as an example of productivity-paradox work. The journal asks what should be measured if the paradox is partly a measurement problem, and what measurement problems AI would cause in sectors that sell people's time, such as advertising and law.
- **Haynes, *Productivity*** (book; no link). Economists treat production as a black box, measuring more inputs and outputs rather than looking inside, which is like reading the ingredients on a ready meal and thinking you know how it was made. The journal uses this to argue that AI isn't just a cheaper input, because major technologies restructure what's possible.
- **Salford analysis sent by RRW** (no link). Plots AI complementarity against AI exposure. The journal questions whether the two axes are really independent, since a job that isn't exposed can't be either complemented or substituted.
- **"The Bandwagon" (1956)**, IEEE. <https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/stamp/stamp.jsp?arnumber=1056774> The journal just flags it ("The bandwagon 1956!"). It's probably Claude Shannon's editorial warning against overenthusiasm for information theory, but I haven't checked.
- **"AI: great expectations" (1988)**. <https://people.csail.mit.edu/brooks/idocs/AI_hype_1988.pdf> An early piece on AI hype, hosted on Rodney Brooks's MIT pages. The journal calls it brilliant and wonders whether its author also wrote a LinkedIn post about AI overhype "37+ years ago" (listed under LinkedIn posts below).

## Data, lookups and classifications

- **Felten et al. AIOE and AIIE scores** (open data from the 2021 paper above). Linked to UK SIC codes by hand, using the most detailed match available, and to UK occupations through a US–UK lookup.
- **NFER, mapping UK SOC 2020 to O\*NET** (Skills Imperative 2035 project, led by Andy Dickerson). <https://www.nfer.ac.uk/key-topics-expertise/education-to-employment/the-skills-imperative-2035/resources/systematic-mapping-of-soc-2020/> and <https://www.nfer.ac.uk/media/1tojrw0o/matching_uk_soc2020_to_o_net.pdf> An open lookup for linking O\*NET-based measures to UK occupations, so the project didn't need to build its own.
- **ONS, SIC/SOC coding of social and business surveys** (FOI response). <https://www.ons.gov.uk/aboutus/transparencyandgovernance/freedomofinformationfoi/sicsoccodingofsocialandbusinesssurveys> Shows that the Labour Force Survey codes both SIC and SOC to 4 digits, though probably only in the microdata.
- **ONS, UK SIC 2007**. <https://www.ons.gov.uk/file?uri=%2Fmethodology%2Fclassificationsandstandards%2Fukstandardindustrialclassificationofeconomicactivities%2Fuksic2007%2Fuksic2007web.pdf> UK SIC 2007 is identical to the EU's NACE Rev. 2 down to 4 digits and consistent with the UN's ISIC Rev. 4.
- **ONS, mapping UK SIC 2007 codes to NAICS** (FOI response). <https://www.ons.gov.uk/aboutus/transparencyandgovernance/freedomofinformationfoi/mapinguksic2007codestonaic> Used to check how well US NAICS codes line up with UK SIC. The journal wasn't convinced the match is as good as it first looked.
- **ONS, SOC 2010 to ISCO-08 crosswalk** (spreadsheet). <https://www.ons.gov.uk/file?uri=/methodology/classificationsandstandards/standardoccupationalclassificationsoc/soc2010/ug201002soc2010toisco08v2_tcm77-283163.xls> Supplied with the DfE report to map the ILO's results onto UK occupations.
- **Census 2021 workplace occupation counts** (no link). Counts by 3-digit SOC (105 categories) for MSOAs and local authorities. The project used them to put AIOE scores on small areas.
- **Companies House** (no link). Gives each firm's SIC codes (up to four) and employee counts. Each firm got an average AIIE score across its SIC codes, which produced the project's first map.
- **BRES** (no link). Considered as another dataset to attach AIIE scores to.
- **NOMIS** (no link). Source for the counts of micro businesses.
- **O\*NET** (no link). The US occupational database behind the Felten, Pizzinelli and Microsoft measures.

## Methods

- **ellmer, "Prompt design"** (tidyverse documentation). <https://ellmer.tidyverse.org/articles/prompt-design.html> Suggests structuring prompts with Markdown. The journal liked Hadley Wickham's line that LLMs are "a sort of an average of the internet at a given point in time", and asks what average picture could be pulled out of them.
- **Ranking from pairwise comparisons** (Oxford course slides, on Mihai Cucuringu's pages). <https://www.stats.ox.ac.uk/~cucuring/CDT_17_Ranking_FoDS.pdf> Rankings built from pairwise comparisons don't need every comparison to be transitive. The aim is an ordering that is as consistent as possible with the data. This supported ranking sectors from the LLM's pairwise judgements with Bradley–Terry models.
- **Tale of Two Countries** (no link, and the journal doesn't say what it is). The source of the idea of summarising places by the proportion of jobs in the top or bottom decile.

## Articles, blogs and commentary

- **Discontinuity Thesis** (website). <https://discontinuitythesis.com/> Argues that AI automates cognition itself, so capitalism's wage-demand engine collapses if AI plus a human checker can beat humans and new jobs or wealth transfers don't arrive fast enough. The journal's reply is that this is just the production-function view, and cognition isn't a simple input. Found via a post by Ben Luong on Sheffield Digital.
- **Diane Coyle** (no link). Her point that the mobile phone has arguably had a deeper economic impact than AI so far, yet has seemed much less visible.
- **Wilkes, "A blob chart way of dissecting Britain's prosperity failure"** (blog, 2023). <https://freethinkecon.wordpress.com/2023/09/28/a-blob-chart-way-of-dissecting-britains-prosperity-failure/> The smartphone, a huge general-purpose technology, hasn't visibly raised productivity in other industries, which is a reason to doubt that AI will transform growth. The journal compares this with Google, the mobile phone and the shift to working from home.
- **Brad DeLong, interim report on his experience of AI** (Substack). <https://braddelong.substack.com/p/an-interim-report-on-my-experience> VisiCalc and Google search changed work by collapsing the time between question and answer. AI browser panes are the next step, because they interpret and summarise as well as fetch.
- **Tim O'Reilly, "Is AI a normal technology?"** (O'Reilly Radar). <https://www.oreilly.com/radar/is-ai-a-normal-technology/> The pace of technological change is set by adoption, which depends on economic, social and infrastructure factors, not by the pace of innovation. The journal wants to combine this with task-level exposure and the low adoption rates (the "95%" not adopting) when judging how vulnerable a sector is.
- **Gary Marcus on GPT-5** (Substack). <https://garymarcus.substack.com/p/gpt-5-overdue-overhyped-and-underwhelming> Quotes findings that LLMs are "sophisticated simulators of reasoning-like text" rather than reasoners, and argues they can't go beyond their training data. The journal notes his mention of neurosymbolic models as something to read up on.
- **Ed Zitron, "haters' guide" post** (Where's Your Ed At). <https://www.wheresyoured.at/the-haters-gui/> Argues that generative AI is "a \$50 billion revenue industry masquerading as a \$1 trillion one", that claims of AI agents replacing labour are false, and that it's a bubble. It also links his earlier piece on the tech media: <https://www.wheresyoured.at/sic/>
- **CNN, Allison Morrow on Dario Amodei's warnings about jobs** (2025). <https://edition.cnn.com/2025/05/30/business/anthropic-amodei-ai-jobs-nightcap> Sojourner, of the Upjohn Institute, says Amodei's scenario implies a 30% jump in labour productivity, when computer adoption in the 1980s and 90s raised it by only 2% to 3%. The journal asks why computers added so little, given how much value the technology seems to have.
- **Nik Suresh, "Contra Ptacek's terrible article on AI"** (Ludicity blog). <https://ludic.mataroa.blog/blog/contra-ptaceks-terrible-article-on-ai/> Live AI programming looked useful for some tedious problems but not orders of magnitude faster, and the hype is at "Amway-Megachurch-Cult levels". He suggests modest experiments, some licences and coaching, rather than transformation. It replies to Thomas Ptacek's "My AI Skeptic Friends Are All Nuts": <https://fly.io/blog/youre-all-nuts/>
- **Financial Times, on-the-job learning**. <https://www.ft.com/content/071089b8-839a-4f96-af79-394c08a146d1> Hybrid working and generative AI together threaten how junior staff learn: hybrid working cuts their time with senior colleagues, and AI removes the routine tasks they used to learn on. The journal asks whether AI's productivity effects will take a generational shift to show up.
- **Guardian interview with futurist Adam Dorr** (2025). <https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2025/jul/09/futurist-adam-dorr-robots-ai-jobs-replace-human-labour> Claims that 1,500-plus technological transformations show a consistent pattern. The journal is very sceptical, and the user posted about it on LinkedIn (see the user's own outputs below).
- **New York Times, "Sketched Out: An Illustrator Confronts His Fears About A.I. Art"** (2025). <https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2025/06/23/magazine/ai-art-artists-illustrator.html> No comment in the journal.
- **Brookings, on Pope Francis, AI and the future of work**. <https://www.brookings.edu/articles/the-unexpected-visionary-pope-francis-on-ai-humanity-and-the-future-of-work/> By the author of the LinkedIn post on low-wage workers listed below. No comment in the journal.
- **The Conversation, review of "The AI Con"**. <https://theconversation.com/is-ai-a-con-a-new-book-punctures-the-hype-and-proposes-some-ways-to-resist-257015> A review of a book arguing that AI is mostly hype. No comment in the journal.
- **Healthcare Business Today, on hallucinations in AI medical summaries**. <https://www.healthcarebusinesstoday.com/hallucinations-in-ai-generated-medical-summaries-why-accuracy-is-essential-in-healthcare/> Found in the comments on the post about Wes Streeting and GPs listed below. It's about why accuracy matters in AI-generated medical summaries.
- **Gateway One, Doncaster** (council news story). <https://www.doncaster.gov.uk/News/tech-leaders-gather-for-groundbreaking-of-doncaster-s-digital-and-tech-hub-gateway-one> A new tech and AI hub whose anchor tenant, Automated Analytics, is registered at Companies House under SIC 82990 (other business support services not elsewhere classified). The journal notes the code, presumably because SIC codes can hide where AI activity is. Related links: the firm's site <https://automatedanalytics.co/us/>, its LinkedIn page <https://www.linkedin.com/company/automated-analyticsuk/> and its Companies House entry <https://find-and-update.company-information.service.gov.uk/company/08803824>.
